# Ideas

children are governed by elected institutions while being excluded from choosing them

## Candidate-authored comprehension tests for children’s voting rights

Proposal: enable children to vote directly in elections after demonstrating understanding of the ballot through a decentralized, AI-administered assessment.

The comprehension requirement applies to children only; adults are exempt. Children must also pass a comprehension assessment to be eligible for election, as described below.

- Each candidate submits 2–3 questions about their stated proposals, with an explanation and an answer rubric, before voting begins.
- Questions assess comprehension, not political agreement. A voter can reject a candidate’s proposals and still pass.
- Public review checks questions for ambiguity, misleading premises, and unequal difficulty.
- Independent providers can administer the assessment using AI and a shared public rubric, with explanations, retries, and independent appeals.
- Candidates supply the material, but do not individually control eligibility. The passing rule is agreed independently of candidates; no candidate has an individual veto through their test.
- A reviewable assessment record documents what was asked and how it was scored. It provides evidence of demonstrated comprehension of the tested material, not proof of every aspect of political understanding.

Open questions: the passing threshold; whether voters answer every candidate’s questions or an equivalent, balanced selection; how to validate scoring across providers; and who governs question review and appeals.

## Children’s right to collect and preserve evidence

Proposed principle: children have the right to collect, preserve, and submit evidence of abuse or rights violations, including those committed by their own family members, teachers, and state authorities, without retaliation.

- No permission from the accused: children should not need the approval of the person or institution concerned to document or report their conduct.
- Independent storage: children can preserve evidence confidentially outside the accused person’s control, with submission receipts and access records.
- Protection against retaliation: threats, punishment, forced deletion, and destruction of evidence should themselves be grounds for investigation.
- Independent reporting routes: children can approach an independent advocate or oversight body, including when allegations concern police or other state authorities.
- Fair assessment: evidence is evaluated on its merits, with an opportunity for the accused to respond.
- Clearly defined scope: documenting personal experiences and preserving received messages are distinct from accessing someone else’s accounts without authorization. Confidential collection and public publication require separate rules.

### Independent evidence deposit service

Create a service where children can preserve material without immediately initiating a complaint, receive a verifiable deposit receipt, and obtain confidential help with next steps. Clearly explain any limits on confidentiality concerning emergencies and mandatory reporting. A timestamp establishes when material was deposited, not whether its contents or accompanying claims are true.

Open questions: independent governance, secure access if a child’s devices are controlled by the accused, confidentiality and reporting rules, retention and deletion, and safeguards against evidence tampering or misuse.

This is a proposed right and institutional design, not a claim that every evidence-gathering or recording method is currently lawful in every jurisdiction.

## Children’s right to a social network without adults

Proposed principle: children have the right to form and govern their own online communities, with protection against adult intrusion, surveillance, impersonation, and commercial exploitation. These spaces should allow children to form friendships, organize, and speak among themselves without routine monitoring by parents, teachers, or advertisers.

- Children-only membership, with privacy-preserving age verification.
- Rules and moderators chosen by members, with ways to appeal moderation decisions.
- No parental monitoring accounts, targeted advertising, or sale of members’ data.
- Help invited by a child through a separate channel that does not grant access to other members’ conversations.
- Explicit, narrow emergency procedures so protection does not become a blanket justification for surveillance.
- Separate membership from infrastructure: adults may maintain servers without permission to browse conversations or participate in the community.
- Allow age-bounded spaces and establish a clear transition when members become adults.

### Privacy-preserving eligibility verification

Explore an independent service that confirms only whether someone is eligible for a community, without giving the network their name, identity documents, or exact birth date. This is a design goal requiring validation, not a guarantee that adult infiltration can be eliminated.

Open questions: reliable eligibility verification without creating a centralized identity database of children; governance and moderation accountability; preventing bullying and impersonation; emergency access boundaries; and members’ data and relationships when they age out.

## Children’s right to own and earn money at any age

Proposed principle: children have the right to own money, earn income, and hold and control their own bank account at any age. Age alone gives no parent, guardian, trustee, or other adult access to or control over the child’s money.

- The child’s earnings belong entirely to the child. No adult acquires a share merely because they are a parent, guardian, adviser, or trustee.
- Earnings, gifts, savings, and royalties can be paid directly into an account solely owned by the child.
- The child controls payments and withdrawals. Parental permission, a joint adult account holder, or an adult trustee should not be conditions of access to banking.
- Assistance can consist of explanations and accessible interfaces; it does not require rights to inspect, withdraw, freeze, or redirect the child’s funds.
- Any delegated access must be voluntarily authorized and revocable by the child, under the same principle as for an adult account holder. There is no automatic category of adult “helpers” with account rights.
- Banks should protect against unauthorized access and provide a complaint process children can use independently. Withheld wages and other money that never reaches the account require a separate enforcement route.
- The right to earn does not authorize forced or dangerous work. Payment does not give an adult the right to compel a child’s participation.

Open question: how to provide banking for someone who cannot yet operate an account themselves, without treating that circumstance as a justification for automatic adult control over children’s accounts generally.

This is a proposed rights framework, not a description of current banking or employment law.

## Children’s right to develop and live by their own ethos

Proposed principle: children have the right to develop, articulate, practice, and revise their own ethos: to understand virtues, establish their ordering, and participate in ethical relationships as persons with an ethos of their own. Parents, institutions, and state authorities must not impose their ethos by force.

### Meaning of ethos and virtues

Based on [On Ethos](docs/Documents/whole-ethos.md) and [On Virtues](docs/Documents/whole-virtues.md):

- Virtues have definitions, relationships, and measurable dimensions, distinguishing virtue from deficiency and excess. They are not simply whatever someone calls good.
- An ethos concerns what ought to be. It includes the virtues a person recognizes, their ordering, and means for others to know, challenge, or adopt it.
- Ethics concerns interaction between ethoses. The minimal ethos recognizes other ethoses as equally important unless demonstrated otherwise; flexible and challengeable ethoses provide mechanisms for reasoned revision.

### Implications for children

- The child’s ethos must count in relationships, including when it challenges an adult’s ethos. The right extends beyond privately holding different beliefs.
- Parental or institutional status alone cannot establish the superiority of an adult’s ethos. This is an application of the framework to children, rather than a claim that the source documents explicitly establish this right.
- Disagreements should address definitions, virtue priorities, reasoning, evidence, and consistency between declarations and conduct, rather than be settled by forced agreement.
- Children can challenge an adult’s or institution’s ordering of virtues and proposed conduct; their own ethos can also be examined through reciprocal ethical challenge.
- Children must be free to revise their ethos through understanding and reasoned engagement, without punishment or withdrawal of necessities for disagreement.

Example: if a school invokes loyalty to stop a child documenting wrongdoing, the child can challenge what justifies ranking loyalty above justice and whether the institution’s conduct is consistent with its declared ethos. The child need not merely request an exception to an unquestionable adult rule.

## Children’s right to a jury of peers

Proposed principle: children have the right to be judged by a jury of peers. In disputes between children and adults, the jury must include both children and adults, with equal representation and equal voting power.

- Independent selection: parents, teachers, and institutions involved in the case cannot choose the jurors.
- Equal authority: child jurors receive the same evidence, can question it, and participate fully in deliberation.
- Accessible proceedings: explanations and assistance enable participation without substituting adults’ judgments for those of child jurors.
- Protection against conflicts and retaliation: jurors must be independent of the parties and free to decide.
- Mixed-jury decision rule: neither age group should be able to impose a verdict alone. Deadlock must lead to a defined review or retrial process.
- Disputes between a child and a school, police force, or other adult-run institution count as child–adult disputes. Institutions cannot avoid a mixed jury by appearing as organizations rather than individuals.

Open questions: how to define and select peers; the decision threshold; the scope of proceedings covered; and procedures for deadlock, review, and retrial.

## Children’s right to travel, including alone

Proposed principle: children have the right to freedom of movement and to travel independently. Any restriction must address a specific, demonstrable risk and be proportionate, explained, and independently challengeable; age alone is insufficient justification.

- Independent access: children can obtain travel documents, buy tickets, and use transport and accommodation without blanket parental-authorization requirements.
- Voluntary assistance: route planning, accessible information, and emergency support are available without mandatory adult accompaniment or parental tracking.
- Protection from confinement: children can challenge being prevented from leaving a home, school, or institution, especially when seeking safety or reporting wrongdoing.
- Individual assessment: someone capable of making a particular journey should not be barred solely because other children might need help.
- Reviewable restrictions: stopping a journey requires stated reasons and a timely appeal. Review must be fast enough to provide a meaningful remedy before the journey becomes impossible.
- Right to return: traveling independently must not be grounds for losing one’s home, care, or access to education.

### Independent travel-rights service

Create a service that helps children obtain travel documents and urgently contest refusals by guardians or travel providers, independently of the person or institution imposing the restriction.

Open questions: assessment of journey-specific risks; urgent review procedures; access to accommodation and emergency support; and international border-entry, custody, and documentation rules.

This is a proposed rights framework, not a description of current travel law.

## Children’s right to sign contracts

Proposed principle: children have the right to negotiate, enter, enforce, and terminate contracts in their own name. Age alone should not invalidate their agreement or transfer contractual authority to an adult. An adult’s signature should not automatically be required for the child’s agreement to have legal effect.

- Understandable terms: the price, obligations, duration, cancellation rules, and consequences of breach must be clearly explained.
- Independent consent: parents cannot contract on the child’s behalf merely because they are parents, or compel the child to sign.
- Direct enforcement: children can demand payment, refunds, or performance and bring disputes themselves.
- Accessible remedies: coercion, deception, and exploitation can be challenged without permission from the person responsible.
- Voluntary advice: assistance is available without making an adviser a compulsory co-signatory or account controller.
- Protections should address the particular risks of an agreement. Ordinary purchases and paid commissions differ from long-term debt, guarantees, or commitments affecting essential rights; age alone is insufficient justification for denying contractual agency.

### Plain-language contract record

Before signing, both parties confirm the same short statement of what each promises, what each receives, and how either can end the agreement. This provides evidence of the disclosed terms, not conclusive proof of understanding or genuine consent.

Open questions: enforcement and dispute procedures; remedies for invalid consent; cancellation and termination rights; and protections for particularly consequential financial or personal commitments.

This is a proposed rights framework, not a description of current contract law.

## Children’s right to privacy, a safe box, and a safe space

Proposed principle: children have the right to privacy in their communications, belongings, and personal space, including access to secure storage and a safe place where they can be alone or seek refuge.

- Private communications: parents and institutions have no automatic entitlement to read messages, diaries, or correspondence, or demand passwords.
- A safe box: children control secure physical and digital storage for documents, money, evidence, personal belongings, and creative work. Adults do not automatically receive duplicate keys or recovery access.
- Personal space: children have somewhere to change, rest, think, and communicate without routine observation, recording, or uninvited entry. Shared housing must still make privacy possible.
- A place of refuge: children can access a safe location outside the control of someone threatening or harming them, without requiring that person’s permission.
- Protection against punishment: exercising privacy must not itself be treated as evidence of wrongdoing.
- Limited emergency intrusion: any intrusion must address a concrete danger, be limited to what is necessary, and be explainable and independently reviewable. Responsibility for a child does not authorize permanent surveillance.
- Voluntary safe space: a safe space must not become a place where adults confine the child.

### Practical provision

Provide a personal lockable compartment at home and school, plus independent secure storage when those places are unsafe. Combine this with access to private personal space and voluntary refuge.

Open questions: provision in shared housing; independent storage and refuge access; secure recovery without automatic adult access; and narrowly defined emergency intervention and review procedures.

## The right to vote and be elected at any age

Proposed principle: people may vote, stand for election, and hold elected office at any age. Children must first pass a comprehension test, both to vote and to be eligible for election. Adults are exempt from these tests.

- No minimum age: age alone must not disqualify a voter, candidate, or elected representative. The comprehension requirement for children is a separate eligibility condition.
- Independent candidacy: no automatic requirement for parental permission or an adult sponsor.
- Equal electoral conditions apart from the specified comprehension requirement: access to nomination procedures, debates, campaigning, and ballots under the same rules.
- Full authority when elected: children exercise the office’s actual powers and responsibilities and receive its remuneration, rather than holding an honorary position controlled by an adult.
- Practical support: accessible procedures and arrangements for education and participation must not transfer the elected person’s authority to a guardian.

### Comprehension assessments

- For voting: demonstrate understanding of the ballot through the candidate-authored assessment proposal above.
- For eligibility to be elected: assess understanding of the office’s powers, duties, and limits. Assess comprehension, not political agreement, without giving opponents control over eligibility.
- Both requirements apply only to children. Passing the voting assessment does not by itself establish understanding of the office.

Open questions: who authors and reviews the office-specific assessment; its passing threshold and timing; and how appeals are completed before nomination or election deadlines.
