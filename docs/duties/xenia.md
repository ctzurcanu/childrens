# Political Xenia

**When you have a voice in a decision, you have a duty to consider those affected who are not speaking.**

Imagine a town deciding whether to build over a pond. The voters can speak. The frogs cannot. People living downstream might not be invited. Children who will live there fifty years from now are not yet born.

A fair decision needs more than the wishes of the people in the room.

We call the duty to bring these missing interests into public decisions **Political Xenia**. It includes those who cannot represent themselves and those who do not want to take part. Their absence must not make their interests disappear.

This document develops a proposed duty for the Children’s International. It is an ethical proposal, not a description of an existing law.

## What does the name mean?

The [virtues collection](/documents/whole-virtues.md) describes **xenia** as hospitality: welcoming and treating those who are far from home with care and respect.

Political Xenia extends that idea to decision-making. We make room for interests that are foreign to our group, missing from our meeting, or unable to speak in our language. This is our proposed use of the term, rather than a claim that the ancient idea already contained this whole political system.

**A simple reminder: “Who is missing, and what do we owe them?”**

## Children have duties as well as rights

Children can ask for their voices to count while also helping other voices count. Being excluded ourselves should make us more alert to exclusion elsewhere.

The duty grows with our ability and responsibility. A child in a class meeting might ask about birds nesting near a proposed play area. An elected representative should investigate effects, hear objections, and explain decisions publicly.

Children do not have to solve every problem or carry this responsibility alone. Adults and institutions have the same duty, with greater responsibilities when they have greater power. Children’s rights are not a prize that adults can withdraw for imperfect performance of a duty.

## Who belongs in our consideration?

### Animals

Animals cannot enter our debates and explain themselves in human words. We should bring evidence about their needs and the effects of our actions: suffering, food, shelter, movement, and the conditions in which they can live.

For example, a road plan should consider animals whose habitat it cuts in two. A representative should examine possible crossings or another route, rather than merely say, “I like animals.”

### Plants, living systems, and whole planets

Plants, forests, rivers, and oceans cannot cast votes. We should consider their continued existence and the relationships that keep living systems healthy, including value beyond their immediate usefulness to us.

Earth as a whole also belongs in the discussion. If our decisions affect other planets, Political Xenia asks us to consider those worlds too: what might be damaged, contaminated, or lost before we understand it?

We do not need to pretend that a tree or planet has human opinions. We must distinguish what evidence tells us about it from the values we use to decide how it should be treated.

### Humans who are not yet born

Future people cannot attend today’s meeting, but they will inherit some of its consequences. Our duty includes considering the water, land, knowledge, resources, institutions, and opportunities we leave them.

We cannot know all their preferences. We can try to preserve their ability to make their own choices and avoid leaving them preventable, lasting damage. The UN’s [Declaration on Future Generations](https://www.un.org/pact-for-the-future/en/declaration-future-generations) provides a related starting point: present decisions should consider future people’s needs and interests.

### Foreign peoples and powers, including rivals

A decision made in one country can affect people in another. Political Xenia asks us to consider those interests even when the countries disagree.

For example, a public debate in the United States should fairly examine relevant interests and stated concerns of Russia and China, as well as those of other affected countries. That includes distinguishing governments, populations, and people who disagree with their own governments. None is a single voice for all the others.

Foreign powers may already have diplomats and considerable influence. The missing element can be fair consideration inside our own decision process, rather than an inability to speak anywhere.

Representing an argument fairly does not require accepting it. Claims must still be checked, and demands can be rejected with reasons. Political Xenia gives no government permission to harm others or control our decisions.

### People who decline participation or reject our political system

Someone may distrust an election, dislike democracy, prefer another way of governing, or simply decline to participate. We still owe them fair consideration when our decisions affect them.

Silence is not agreement. Refusing our invitation does not make someone’s home, safety, or interests less important. But it also does not authorize us to invent their wishes.

Native American nations illustrate the need to respect self-government and different political traditions. They should not be described generally as people who do not appreciate democracy: many tribal governments have elected leadership, alongside continuing traditional systems. See [How do tribal members govern themselves?](https://www.bia.gov/faqs/how-do-tribal-members-govern-themselves).

The practical duty is to listen to the particular nation and its own chosen representatives. Declining to join an outside political process must not be treated as an invitation to replace its institutions.

## Representing interests without taking over

There are three different situations:

1. **Someone asks you to represent them.** Agree on what you may say and decide. They should be able to correct you or end that permission.
2. **Someone can speak but does not participate.** Invite their contribution without pressure. Respect refusals. Explain effects on them, but do not claim their support or invent a position for them.
3. **Someone cannot authorize a representative.** Animals, future humans, and living systems need advocates who present evidence, explain uncertainty, and allow others to challenge their conclusions.

An **advocate** makes a case for someone’s interests. Being an advocate does not automatically make you their official spokesperson or give you power over them.

For example, say, “This proposal may damage the river because…” rather than, “The river has chosen me and agrees with my whole plan.”

## Six things the duty asks us to do

1. **Notice.** Identify who or what may be affected, including beyond our borders and lifetimes.
2. **Listen.** Seek affected people’s own accounts and chosen representatives. Make participation easier where possible.
3. **Learn.** Use relevant evidence. Separate established facts, estimates, personal judgments, and unknowns.
4. **Present fairly.** Explain the strongest supported concerns, including concerns inconvenient to your own side. Do not hide disagreements within a group.
5. **Seek a better decision.** Look for ways to prevent harm, meet competing needs, and preserve future choices. Explain conflicts that cannot be fully resolved.
6. **Answer for your work.** Record your reasons, accept corrections, and check what happens after the decision.

Good intentions alone are not enough. Someone who claims to protect a forest should be willing to show how their proposal affects it.

## A practical method: the empty-chair review

A class, community, or council could reserve time before important decisions to consider the absent. An empty chair can remind everyone of the task; several advocates may be needed because the interests differ.

Before deciding, complete this short record:

| Question | What to record |
| --- | --- |
| Who is affected but missing? | Name the people, living beings, places, or future generations. |
| Why are they missing? | Cannot participate, were excluded, declined, or have not been contacted. |
| Who may speak for them? | Identify permission where it exists; otherwise state the limits of the advocacy. |
| What do we know? | Give evidence, direct statements, disagreements, and uncertainties. |
| What alternatives exist? | Compare likely benefits, harms, and ways to reduce harm. |
| What did we decide, and why? | Explain how the missing interests changed the decision, or why an objection was not accepted. |
| When will we check again? | Name a review time and a way to report mistakes or unexpected harm. |

Under this proposed process, the decision-makers must respond to the concerns. Merely allowing an advocate to speak and then ignoring them would not fulfil the duty.

## Example: a playground beside a pond

Children want more room to play. An empty-chair review identifies frogs, plants, downstream neighbours, and future children as affected.

The group asks neighbours for their views, seeks evidence about the pond, and compares building over it with using another site. It does not claim that every neighbour agrees or that it knows exactly what future children will want.

The group might choose a smaller playground that preserves the pond. If it chooses another option, it must still explain the harms, its reasons, and how those harms will be addressed. Later, it checks whether its expectations were correct.

Political Xenia does not promise a choice that pleases everyone. It requires that those missing from the vote matter in the decision.

## Keeping the duty honest

- **No invented consent:** do not turn silence into permission or claim to represent a whole people without authority.
- **No automatic veto:** raising a concern does not settle the decision. Serious objections deserve evidence and an answer.
- **No hidden interests:** disclose who selected, funds, or benefits from an advocate’s work.
- **No permanent ownership of a voice:** allow competing advocates, correction, and replacement. Make room for people to speak for themselves when they choose.
- **No requirement to agree with us first:** consideration is owed even to those who do not return it. Their own conduct remains open to criticism.
- **No selective compassion:** include unpopular outsiders as well as appealing causes. Consider vulnerable people within powerful countries, too.

## Questions we still need to settle

- How should advocates be chosen when those represented cannot choose them?
- What resources would allow children to investigate a concern properly?
- Which decisions should require a formal empty-chair review?
- How should competing duties to animals, plants, present people, and future people be weighed?
- Who should hear a complaint that a missing interest was ignored or misrepresented?
- What consequences should follow deliberate deception or repeated neglect by someone holding public responsibility?

## A short commitment

> When I help make a decision, I will ask who is missing. I will listen where I can, learn what I can, and explain what I do not know. I will bring their interests into the discussion without pretending to own their voice. I will accept correction and help check the consequences.
